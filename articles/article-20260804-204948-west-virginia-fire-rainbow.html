What Exactly IS a "Fire Rainbow"?

Scientifically known as a circumhorizontal arc, a "fire rainbow" isn't caused by rain or fire at all. Instead, it's a type of ice halo formed by the refraction of sunlight through plate-shaped ice crystals suspended in high-altitude cirrus clouds. For this dazzling display to occur, several precise conditions must align. The sun needs to be very high in the sky – at least 58 degrees above the horizon. The ice crystals, typically hexagonal and flat, must be oriented horizontally, acting like millions of tiny prisms.

A Symphony of Science and Sunlight

The rarity of a circumhorizontal arc lies in this delicate dance of atmospheric elements. Most rainbows we see are formed by sunlight refracting through water droplets. Fire rainbows, however, require those specific ice crystals and that particular sun angle, making them far less common. When all these factors synchronize, the sun's rays enter through one vertical side of the hexagonal crystal and exit through the bottom, bending and separating into a spectrum of colors. The result is a brilliant, iridescent band that appears parallel to the horizon, often resembling a fiery, horizontal rainbow.
